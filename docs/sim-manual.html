I.The tick & the pipeline

Decides: nothing — this is the clockwork the actors run inside. Order is load-bearing.

The server owns the whole world. Every real tick (20 Hz), a clock turns real elapsed time × the speed dial into dtSim sim-seconds (0 when paused). The sim then advances the world in fixed 0.05 s substeps — so 10× fast-forward is literally ten 1× steps, identical dynamics at every speed world.js:182. A single ordered list of 21 systems runs once per substep systems.js:27; the order below is exact, and it matters — production happens before pricing, pricing before dispatch, and every fleet moves before the shore guns and the collision solver read final positions.

The client is a pure viewer: it merges server snapshots by id and never simulates SimClient.js:7. The only authoritative thing a viewer can change today is the clock.

II.The actors, at a glance

Decides: who holds a will in this world, and what levers each one pulls.

There is no single "AI." Behaviour is distributed across independent agents, each mutating its own slice of state on its own trigger. Two of them mirror each other exactly: a ship's crew (morale → mutiny, gated by the captain's Command) and an island's populace (loyalty → rebellion, gated by the magistrate's skill) are the same machine at two scales.

III.The Port — the economy engine

Decides: what a port makes, what it charges, and whether its people eat or breed. Runs blind (no will) — pure functions of stock and population.
  • Produce. Each island mines its primary raw at 10/s and secondary at ×0.25, with a logistic taper toward a 2000 stockpile cap production.js:17. Recipe goods scale to population (0.07 × pop) but are clamped so ≥25% of any local raw survives as exportable surplus — this is what forces trade production.js:46-62.
  • Consume. Population eats 0.011 food/head, plus comfort goods (Ale, Clothing, Weapons, Luxury) burned as demand sinks so every port keeps importing population.js:28-41.
  • Live or die. Starvation deaths are rate-bounded and floored at pop 5; growth is gated on food security (2+ days stored) so population can never outrun its larder population.js:44-57. There is no true death-spiral here.
  • Price. Mid price = base × clamp((target÷stock)^1, 0.33, 3.0), eased toward target by a non-overshooting 10%/s limiter; bid/ask are ±5% pricing.js:13-26.
  • Bank. Income = 2.4 × pop × (0.4 + 0.6·civ); sinks are ship upkeep + a per-capita admin cost, and any gold above 40 × pop overflows and is destroyed. Gold is deliberately not conserved upkeep.js:38-41.

Trap

Food is spoiled twice per substep. population decays Food by 0.0003·h and then upkeep decays all stock — Food included — by another 0.0004·h population.js:32 · upkeep.js:45. Food loses ~2.3× the decay rate of every other good, tightening the very margin that drives the "starving tail."

Sound

Starvation is floored and growth is food-gated, so no runaway death-spiral. Every good/gold trade move goes through the conserved transfer primitive — the only sanctioned gold source/sink is upkeep (plus development & contract escrow).

IV.The Port — buying hulls ◆ mostly broken

Decides: how a port replaces lost ships. Two independent paths — and both are throttled by a mispriced constant.

A port grows its fleet two ways: opportunistically, by sending an idle hull to a shipyard on a voyage; or reliably, via development, which spawns a hull at home each day if the treasury is fat. The intent is sound. The execution is undermined by a pricing bug that makes the true cost of a ship ~3× what every gate assumes.

Bug · high

Ships never cost their base price. SHIP_STOCK_CAP = 3 caps a yard's stock.Ships, but the price target for Ships defaults to 250, so (250÷3) saturates the multiplier at the 3.0 ceiling always economy.json:88 · pricing.js:14. Ask ≈ 3780g, not the advertised 1200. Every downstream threshold keyed to 1200 is wrong by ~3×.

Deadlock · high

Small ports can never replace a hull. Healthy treasuries pin at the 40 × pop hoard cap. Buying needs ~4780g ⇒ pop ≥ ~120; starting pop is 80. So a port must nearly double its population before it can afford one ship — while pirates and storms take its starting hulls in the meantime. This is the structural engine behind the known "starving tail." upkeep.js:40 · trade.js:33 · development.js:40

V.The Magistrate — governance, loyalty & rebellion

Decides: a port's economic ambition (which becomes its import/export demand), and whether the populace stays loyal or burns the docks.

Each island is ruled by a named magistrate with three traits (firmness, generosity, integrity) and one of six agendas — grow, industry, wealth, fortify, splendor, order — chosen by the island's nature magistrate.js:69-85. The agenda retargets the economy: it rewrites the port's targets[] (fortify boosts Weapons ×2.4, splendor Luxury ×2.2, etc.), and those targets are the port's dynamic demand that the trade planner reads. Food is always floored at base — no agenda can starve the port magistrate.js:114-125.

Loyalty eases toward a steady value set by civ, skill, generosity and lawlessness, dragged down by famine, plague, crowding and graft, buoyed by "bread & circuses" (Ale+Luxury in stock) magistrate.js:258-266. Below loyalty 0.25, unrest accrues; once it passes a skill-scaled grace window, the island rebels — halting all production and income for exactly one day — then resolves to a quell or an overthrow magistrate.js:272-318.

Sound

Rebellions always start when warranted and always end after exactly one day (resolution is unconditional). Overthrow vents grievance (×0.4) and haven-redemption caps it at 0.25, so an island is never permanently ungovernable — though the recovery road may run through becoming a haven first. The lawlessness↔civ loop converges (L≈0.27) rather than running away; it only tips to haven territory under an external shock (piracy danger).

Trap · med

Some agendas are structurally unwinnable and quietly bleed loyalty. A pure shipyard makes only "special" goods, so industry's signal is pinned at −1 forever — yet the agenda-chooser actively pushes shipyards toward industry magistrate.js:75,142-145. Likewise order takes a loyalty penalty for the very lawlessness it was installed to fix, and wealth is unreachable for a shrunken port (needs 2000g vs a hoard cap that may be 200).

Deadlock · med

Haven-cap rebellion loop. When havens sit at their 8% cap, a fully-failed island cannot fall — but the haven is its only reset for grievance/lawlessness. It stays in an endless overthrow-every-5-days churn, shedding civ and gold each cycle, with no exit until a haven slot frees up havens.js:96.

VI.The Merchant Captain — planning a voyage

Decides: the single richest decision in the sim — an idle hull is handed a whole multi-stop basket, chosen by a strict priority ladder over the port's needs and the captain's personality.

When a ship falls idle at home, dispatch hands it to planVoyage (with a re-plan cooldown so idle hulls don't re-scan every substep) trade.js:44-78. The planner spends three budgets — hold space, 0.75 × treasury, and a per-good cap — down a fixed ladder. Every price it reasons about is a belief (stale rumour, §XV), not truth; only availability (who has stock/gold) is live. The captain's traits tune the bars: greed raises the minimum-profit floor, boldness widens range and shrinks provisioning, wanderlust drives scouting.

VII.The Ship — states & motion

Decides: moment to moment, where the hull points and how it gets there without hitting land or other ships.

A merchant cycles idle → outbound → trading → inbound → idle, but four orthogonal flags pre-empt that switch: a crew uprising (dead in the water), being blown adrift, fleeing a raider, and sheltering in port. The ship system moves merchants only; pirates and privateers are moved by their own systems — which is why there are three byte-identical copies of the mover, kept in sync by hand to dodge an import cycle.

VIII.The Crew — morale & mutiny

Decides: whether a crew keeps working, deserts to another port, or raises the black flag. Gated at every step by the captain's Command.

Crews eat food and drink ale (a master's crew eats less and cheers harder). At sea, morale eases toward a steady ~0.6, dragged by sea-fatigue and — decisively — by an empty larder. A fed crew mathematically never mutinies: steady-state morale sits well above the 0.3 mutiny line. Trouble only begins when food runs out crew.js:130-144. Two exits end at the black flag: a defeated mutiny may install a new pirate master (stat-driven — likelier the hungrier the crew and the more lawless its home port crew.js:178-186), while — quite apart from any crisis — a bold, greedy captain of a still-loyal crew may hoist it himself and lead them over (maybeTurnRogue, weighed each substep for candidates above the mutiny line; see Fig. IXa) piracy.js:117-136.

IX.The Pirate — the hunt

Decides: a pirate's every move via a strict priority ladder, coloured by a fearsome captain's traits and sharpened by hunger.

Any hull can raise the black flag — a seeded raider, a haven's forge, a prize capture, a mutiny that turns rogue, a fallen haven's home fleet, or an honest captain's own turn to piracy (maybeTurnRogue, weighed each substep for a genuinely bold + greedy master) — always gated by canTurnPirate, which caps pirates at 8% of the fleet piracy.js:99-115. How she came to it decides who commands: the crew either keep their captain as their pirate leader (a rogue turn — he's hardened bold+greedy but keeps his name, portrait, and log-voice, an unbroken fall from honest trade) or throw him over for a fresh, fearsome master (a seizure). A seeded/built raider and a mutiny are always a new master; an organic turn rolls lead-vs-cast-out from the captain's boldness + greed + crew morale — so the self-turn, born of that same boldness+greed, comes up rogue almost always piracy.js:104-171. A pirate then runs the ladder below every substep. Hunger is the master dial: a hungry pirate never rests, uses full hunting range even mid-blockade, and drops its greedy captain's minimum-prize bar to zero.

X.Combat — how a fight resolves

Decides: who wins a broadside exchange, and whether the loser sinks, strikes her colours, or is boarded and taken as a prize.

Combat is attrition, not instant. Each round (every 1.2 s while in range) both ships exchange fire; damage scales with the strength ratio and is divided by hull armour. Doctrine differs by flag: pirates chain-shot the rig to cripple-and-board, the navy pounds the hull to sink, merchants shoot rig to flee piracy.js:364-382. Fighting strength blends a base, gunnery skill (the dominant term, ×1.2), morale, gun count, boldness, a flag bonus, ship class and hull condition piracy.js:78-96. Between exchanges each captain re-weighs the fight (assessFlee): break off only when hull and odds have both turned against her — later the bolder and more seasoned the captain, never if fearless, and never if dismasted (no sail to run under). It is the same running assessment a hunted pirate and a losing privateer both make, every round piracy.js:453-471.

XI.The Navy — danger, bounty & privateers

Decides: the economy's self-balancing answer to piracy — how feared waters get, who funds a hunter, and how that hunter behaves.

An attack marks nearby waters with danger (which decays daily and reroutes merchants), and the aggrieved port posts a bounty from its treasury. Separately, ports commission privateers when a threat is in range and they can afford it — the fleet size scales as 2.5 × pirates + 2 × havens antipiracy.js:71-99. A commissioned privateer arms up, then hunts, patrols, or besieges a haven, breaking off to resupply for free at its guard port when hungry or battered. Like the raider it hunts, it also re-weighs each round whether to keep fighting (assessFlee): a hunter shot up by a heavily-gunned pirate disengages — clearing its prey and keeping clear for a COMBAT_BREAKOFF_RESPITE before it will re-engage — and limps home to refit rather than trading to the bottom antipiracy.js:134 · 315-321.

Balance · med

A lone privateer can't reliably break a haven. Assault is throttled to one −0.11 hit per striker per day, while the haven's shore guns + return fire drive a single besieger below its refit threshold in ~1.8 days and it entrenches +0.01/day. Oscillation · med

Privateers out-number (2.5:1) and out-run pirates, so they wipe them below the floor; the rogue-floor then respawns a batch, which re-triggers commissioning, which wipes them again. The system churns spawn → commission → kill → stand-down rather than settling into a stable pirate population — the floor papers over "piracy went invisible" rather than balancing it.

XII.The Haven — a port gone rogue

Decides: when a failed island stops being governed and becomes a pirate base — and how it's eventually retaken.

A port that stays lawless (≥0.58) and uncivilised (civ ≤0.34) for five days falls: it drops its magistrate, seeds three of its own idle crews as pirates, and starts a strength meter at 0.85. Thereafter it entrenches daily, forges fresh pirates when it can afford the wood and iron, fences plundered goods from visiting pirates, and refits them for free. It's redeemed only when a privateer's repeated assaults grind its strength to zero havens.js.

XIV.The Weather

Decides: seasonal production swings, where storms roam and who they sink, and the single global wind every sail reads.

Seasons rotate every 15 days, swinging food-crop output hardest (winter ×0.78 → autumn ×1.28) and setting the storm rate and prevailing wind. The wind is one global vector easing toward periodically-rerolled targets, floored so a headwind slows but never stalls a fleet weather.js · wind.js.

XV.The Information layer

Decides: what each port and ship knows — because prices don't teleport; they ride the ships. This is a genuine, load-bearing fog of price, not decoration.

A "belief" is a price book tagged with the day it was seen. Islands hold one; ships carry their own. When a ship docks it observes the port's live prices firsthand, and reports its own firsthand sightings of everywhere else — but never re-spreads the port's hearsay. That firsthand-only rule is what keeps knowledge geographic instead of saturating the map beliefs.js:82-105. Beliefs age toward base over ~20 days; the same machinery carries five non-price facts (danger, haven, food, civ, lawlessness), refreshed by sighting islands at sea every substep intel.js.

Crucially, the planner reasons on beliefs (partner choice, purse sizing, migration, scouting) while settlement at the dock uses live prices — so a port can be wrong about a distant price but is never wrong about whether that port has stock or gold (availability is deliberate common knowledge). A parallel voyage ledger lets a home port expect each ship until it's overdue, then presume it lost.

Leak · high

A captured merchant inflates its home's believed fleet forever. When a pirate takes a prize it sets pirate = true but keeps the ship's homeId and id. So the home's fleet census counts the turned-pirate hull as a still-owned trader — no "lost" event ever fires, and the port stops ordering replacements for ships it thinks it still owns (which are now the raiders attacking it). A port bled by capture rather than sinking silently withers piracy.js:429 · voyages.js:57 · fleet.js:15.

Dead agency · low

The setVoyage intent path is fully wired and validated — but no client message reaches it. In "pass 1" a viewer can only change the clock; the ownership/idle checks in intents.js are exercised only by a unit test. Real player agency over ships needs a new protocol message. simHost.js:146-154
